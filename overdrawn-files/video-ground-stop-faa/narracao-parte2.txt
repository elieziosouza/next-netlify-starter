So who pays? In the original FAA-funded study, about half of the cost of delays fell on passengers, not airlines. Missed meetings. Extra hotel nights. Lost work hours. A wedding you didn't make it to.

Airlines pay for crews, fuel burned while waiting, rebooking, and hotel vouchers. And then there's everyone else. Hotels, rental cars, and the businesses waiting for people who never arrived.

One more thing passengers should know. When the cause is an air traffic control problem, and not the airline itself, U.S. airlines generally don't have to cover your hotel or your meals. Some do it anyway. Check your airline's policy before you assume.

But there is one thing you are owed, no matter whose fault it was. Since 2024, U.S. rules require airlines to give you an automatic cash refund if your flight is canceled, or significantly changed, and you choose not to travel. Significantly changed means, for a domestic flight, leaving or arriving more than three hours late. For an international flight, more than six.

And the money has to come back fast. Within seven business days if you paid by credit card. Within twenty days for other payment methods. Not a voucher. Not a travel credit, unless you accept one. Cash, back to the way you paid.

So if your flight was canceled on Friday and you decided to stay home, don't let anyone talk you into a credit you didn't ask for.
